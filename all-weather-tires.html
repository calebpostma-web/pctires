All-Weather Tires in Chatham-Kent: The Tire Built for Here

If you drive to work, to church, to the bank and to the grocery store around Chatham-Kent, an all-weather tire is about as close to ideal as it gets. It stops well in the snow, it grips in the cold, and it stays on your car all year.

Here's what I tell customers at the counter in Pain Court:

  • Driving north to Barrie or cottage country every winter? Get a dedicated winter tire.
  • Spending a lot of time on the highway in Florida or Vegas? A good all-season is probably your best bet.
  • Driving around Chatham-Kent and southwestern Ontario? An all-weather tire is the one I'd put on my own car. It's almost like an all-weather tire was built for this part of Ontario.

They Stop Well in the Snow

All-weather tires carry the 3PMSF mountain-snowflake symbol. To earn it, a tire has to pass a standardized severe-snow traction test, the same certification winter tires carry. Regular all-season tires don't have it. The "M+S" on their sidewall is a tread-pattern label, not a winter test.

The snowflake shows up in braking tests. In Tire Rack's 2026 winter testing, the best all-weather tire (the Bridgestone WeatherPeak) stopped on snow in 58.6 feet. The best dedicated winter tire, the Michelin X-Ice Snow, stopped in 56.1 feet. That's a gap of about 4%, a couple of feet, from a tire you never have to take off.

They Stop Well in the Cold

Snow isn't the whole story. Most of a Chatham-Kent winter is cold, bare or wet pavement, and that's where tire rubber matters most.

All-season rubber starts to stiffen once temperatures drop below about 7°C. Stiff rubber doesn't conform to the road, so the tire grips less and takes longer to stop, even when the road is dry. All-weather tires use a compound that stays pliable in the cold, the same idea as a winter tire. You get better grip from the first cold morning in November, not just when it snows.

Why it fits Chatham-Kent. We're flat, we're on the mild end of Ontario winters, and our roads get salted hard. Most winter days here mean cold pavement, slush and the odd snowfall, not weeks of packed snow. That's exactly where all-weathers do their best work. The one place dedicated winters still win clearly is glare ice, so if you're regularly out on unplowed back roads before the trucks, talk to us about winters.

One Set, All Year

With all-weathers there's no spring and fall changeover, no second set of tires or rims to buy, and nothing to store in the garage. You also don't get caught out by an early storm before your swap appointment.

The good ones are built to last, too. Nexen's Roadian HTX2, an all-weather for SUVs and pickups, carries a 115,000 km treadwear warranty on passenger (P-metric) sizes. That's a long-life warranty on a tire that's snowflake-rated.

On a Budget? Skip the Cheap All-Season

This is the mistake I see most. Someone wants one set of tires for the whole year, wants to spend as little as possible, and buys the cheapest all-season they can find. A cheap all-season performs very poorly in the cold. The rubber stiffens up, and the tire doesn't bite on cold pavement or in snow. A pricier all-season, say a Michelin, holds up a little better in the cold, but it's still an all-season with no snowflake.

If you want one tire for the whole year, want to keep it on budget, and still want to be safe on the road, a budget all-weather checks all three boxes.

Let's be honest: a budget all-weather is not as good as a Michelin CrossClimate2. In my opinion that might be the best tire on the road. But a value all-weather is still significantly better in the cold and snow than a low-priced, discount all-season, for about the same money.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are all-weather tires good enough for a Chatham-Kent winter?

For most drivers here, yes. They carry the 3PMSF mountain-snowflake, so they've passed a real severe-snow traction test, and in independent snow braking tests the best all-weathers stop within a few percent of the best winter tires. Our roads are flat and salted hard, which keeps most winter driving on cold pavement and slush, and that's exactly where all-weathers do well.

What's the difference between all-weather and all-season tires?

Two things. All-weathers carry the 3PMSF snowflake and all-seasons don't (M+S on its own is just a tread-shape label). And all-weathers use a rubber compound that stays soft below about 7°C, while all-season rubber stiffens in the cold and loses grip, even on dry roads.

Do I still need a seasonal changeover with all-weather tires?

No. That's a big part of the appeal: one set stays on all year. No spring and fall swap, no second set of rims, nothing to store. You just keep up regular rotations and pressure checks.

Do all-weather tires qualify for the Ontario winter tire insurance discount?

Ontario auto insurers have to offer a discount for winter tires, but whether a 3PMSF all-weather counts is up to each insurer. Some accept them, some want dedicated winters. Ask your broker before you count on it.

When should I buy winter tires instead?

If you regularly drive north into the snow belt (Barrie, cottage country), or you're out on unplowed rural roads before the salt trucks get there. Dedicated winter tires still stop shorter on glare ice. On the other side, if you do a lot of highway driving somewhere hot like Florida or Vegas, a good all-season wears better in sustained heat.

The Bottom Line

For everyday driving in Chatham-Kent and southwestern Ontario, an all-weather tire gives you near-winter stopping in the snow, real grip in the cold, and one set that stays on all year. If you head north often, go winter. If you live on the highway somewhere hot, go all-season. For everybody else around here, all-weather is the answer.
